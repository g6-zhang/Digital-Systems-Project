Bear

The wild boar (scientific name: Sus scrofa), also known as the feral pig, belongs to the genus Sus. It is native to Eurasia and Africa, but due to human introduction, it has become widespread across the world, adapting to various habitats as an omnivorous species.

The domestic pig, one of the primary sources of meat today, was domesticated from the wild boar approximately 8,000 years ago and is classified as a subspecies of the wild boar. Despite this close relationship, wild boars differ significantly from domestic pigs in both appearance and growth rate—they grow much slower and tend to be lighter in weight than their domesticated counterparts.

The wild boar has a robust body, a large head, and well-developed limbs. Its fur is coarse and sparse, typically dark brown or black, though regional variations exist. Older wild boars develop a patch of white hair on their backs, and in Central Asia, some white-coated wild boars have been observed. Juvenile wild boars have light brown fur with dark stripes. Their backs are covered in stiff bristles, which grow denser in winter for insulation.

On average, wild boars measure 1.5 to 2 meters in body length (excluding the tail), with a shoulder height of around 90 cm. Their weight varies significantly by region, ranging from 90 to 200 kg, though some individuals exceed 200 kg. In Northeast China and Russia's Far East, wild boars can reach nearly 400 kg, making them among the largest of their kind.Male wild boars possess two pairs of continuously growing tusks that serve as both weapons and digging tools. These canine teeth average 6 cm in length, with about 3 cm protruding from the mouth. Female wild boars have shorter, non-protruding tusks, but they are still capable of inflicting damage when defending themselves.
