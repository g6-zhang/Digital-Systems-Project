Dolphins, historically known in China as "fish beasts" (鱼兽), "fish raccoons" (鱼貍), "sea swine" (海狶/海豨), and "dolphin fish" (海豚鱼), are a group of small toothed whales, primarily referring to species in the family Delphinidae (scientific name: Delphinidae). In a broader sense, the term can also include porpoises (Phocoenidae), river dolphins, and various extinct small toothed whales, such as Kentriodontidae, Eurhinodelphinidae, and Waipatiidae. Additionally, pilot whales and killer whales, which are larger members of the dolphin family, are technically dolphins, though they are commonly excluded from the term in non-scientific contexts due to their larger size.

Dolphins are widely distributed across shallow waters near continental shelves in all major oceans. Some species can be found in semi-salty estuaries, and a few inhabit freshwater rivers. Dolphins are highly social animals, often living in pods and preying on fish and squid. Their bodies are streamlined, their skin is smooth and hairless, and they are extremely agile and fast swimmers, capable of leaping and deep diving.

Dolphins possess a highly developed echolocation system, relying primarily on sonar navigation to detect objects and communicate. They have excellent hearing both underwater and in air. Their blowholes, located on the top of their heads, allow them to breathe when surfacing. Dolphin body sizes range from the 1-meter-long, 30-kilogram vaquita to the 9.5-meter-long, 14-ton orca (killer whale), with significant variations in physical features.

Dolphins are among the most intelligent animals, with complex brain structures and strong memory. Many species can learn and perform tasks under human training and are known for their gentle, playful, and social nature, making them widely beloved around the world.
